Methodology & assumptions

How the 4%-rule withdrawal is adjusted for retirement age

The classic 4% rule (Bengen, 1994) assumes a ~30-year retirement starting at 65. There's no single official formula for adjusting it to other ages, so this tool uses a transparent heuristic: withdrawal rate = 4.0% + 0.10% for every year your retirement age is above 65 (and −0.10% for every year below), floored at 2.5% and capped at 6.0%. Retiring earlier means a longer time the portfolio has to last, so a lower starting withdrawal rate is more conservative; retiring later shortens that horizon. Treat this as a starting point, not a guarantee — actual safe withdrawal rates depend heavily on market conditions at retirement (sequence-of-returns risk), portfolio composition, and how much of a legacy you want to leave.

How the Social Security estimate works

This uses the real, public SSA benefit formula — but a simplified proxy for your earnings history, since this tool only knows your current salary, not your actual 35-year record. It projects your salary backward to your career-start age and forward to retirement using your assumed salary growth above inflation, caps each year at the Social Security taxable maximum ($184,500 for 2026), takes your highest 35 years (zero-filling any missing years), and divides by 420 months to get your Average Indexed Monthly Earnings (AIME).

AIME then runs through the actual 2026 bend-point formula: 90% of the first $1,226 of AIME, 32% of the next amount up to $7,391, and 15% above that, giving your Primary Insurance Amount (PIA) at Full Retirement Age (which depends on your birth year). Your chosen Social Security claiming age (62–70, set near the bottom of the form) then adjusts that PIA up or down from Full Retirement Age: reduced by 5/9% per month for the first 36 months claimed early, 5/12% per month beyond that, or increased 2/3% per month (i.e. 8%/year) for each month claimed after Full Retirement Age, up to 70. If you retire before you claim, that gap is a bridge period your portfolio has to cover on its own — the tool flags it separately. The result is presented in today's dollars.

How taxes are estimated

The income figures use 2026 federal tax law only (no state or local taxes) and are computed in today's dollars using today's brackets and standard deduction — a reasonable simplification since both are adjusted for inflation annually and roughly move together over time. Traditional-account and ESOP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income; Roth withdrawals are entirely tax-free; taxable-brokerage withdrawals are taxed only on their embedded gain (tracked from your contributions vs. growth), at long-term capital-gains rates, stacked on top of your ordinary income. The taxable share of your Social Security benefit is estimated using the IRS's combined-income test (50%/85% thresholds).

In retirement, this tool draws down your four account types proportionally to their share of your balance each year — a simplification for illustration, and the basis for the year-by-year table below. Once you reach the age Required Minimum Distributions kick in, if the RMD on your Traditional or ESOP balance exceeds what proportional drawdown would have taken, the tool forces the larger RMD amount out (as the IRS requires) and reinvests anything beyond your target spending, after tax, into your taxable account. In practice, a tax-efficient withdrawal order (often taxable, then traditional and ESOP, then Roth last) can meaningfully reduce lifetime taxes; a financial advisor or tax professional can help sequence this properly.

How your planning age (life expectancy) is set

The “plan until age” field defaults to your current age plus your remaining life expectancy from the SSA's Period Life Table, interpolated for your current age and the sex you select. That table is a median — roughly half of people at any given age go on to live longer than it predicts — so it's a reasonable starting point, not a guarantee, and the field is fully editable. If you'd rather plan conservatively (i.e. make sure your money doesn't run out even if you live longer than average), raise it; family longevity, health, and lifestyle are all things a life expectancy table can't see.

How Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) are modeled

Under SECURE 2.0, RMDs on Traditional and ESOP balances begin at age 75 if you were born in 1960 or later, or age 73 if born earlier — this tool derives your birth year from your current age and applies the correct start age automatically. Each year at or past that age, the required withdrawal is your prior-year-end balance in that bucket divided by the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table divisor for your age (e.g. 26.5 at 73, falling toward 6.4 by age 100). If your target spending already calls for more than the RMD, nothing changes; if the RMD is larger, the extra is withdrawn, taxed, and the after-tax remainder is added to your taxable account rather than spent — a simplification, since real RMD-driven reinvestment decisions depend on your broader financial picture. Roth accounts have no RMDs under current law, which this tool reflects.

How the ESOP is modeled

An Employee Stock Ownership Plan is an employer-funded retirement plan, not a payroll deferral — the company contributes shares or cash to buy shares on your behalf, typically allocated across participants roughly in proportion to compensation, and taxed as ordinary income when distributed (like a 401(k)), which is why this tool treats ESOP withdrawals the same way it treats Traditional withdrawals. Two things distinguish it from a 401(k) balance here: it grows at your own assumed rate for your company's stock, not the broad-market return you set elsewhere (company-specific results can run well above or below the S&P 500); and its contribution rate decays over time by the “allocation decay” percentage you set. That decay is a modeling simplification for a real, well-documented dynamic — as companies mature, employee headcount tends to grow faster than the share pool set aside for the ESOP, so each individual's allocation as a share of pay tends to shrink over the years even as the plan overall keeps growing; newer/smaller/fast-growing companies often see this more sharply than mature ones. There's no single public formula for that decay rate, so the default (3%/year) is a reasonable illustrative starting point, not a sourced constant — adjust it if you know your company's actual allocation trend. This tool does not model vesting schedules or cliffs (grants are treated as immediately vested), single-company concentration risk (an ESOP is far less diversified than a broad index, and a real plan should treat that risk separately from this tool's constant-return assumption), or the specific rules around ESOP distributions at separation vs. retirement.

How the Social Security claiming-age recommendation works

This tool runs your entire retirement simulation — income, RMDs, taxes, account balances — once for every claiming age from 62 through 70, and scores each run by adding up your total after-tax spendable income across every retirement year plus whatever's left in your accounts at death (so a strategy that leaves more unspent legacy isn't penalized, but reinvested RMD dollars aren't double-counted). The age with the highest score is the recommendation. This approach tends to favor delaying Social Security when your other assets can comfortably bridge the gap and you have an average or longer life expectancy (delayed claiming locks in a permanently larger, inflation-protected check for the rest of your life), and favors claiming earlier when a shorter planning horizon or heavier reliance on Social Security for near-term spending changes the math. It is a mathematical optimization only — it doesn't know your health, other income sources, marital/survivor benefit coordination, or personal preference for certainty over a larger eventual check, all of which are legitimate reasons to choose differently.

Dollar figures & the 1996 comparison

“Nominal” dollars are the actual future balance with no inflation adjustment. “Today's purchasing power” deflates that using your assumed inflation rate. The 1996 figure goes one step further: The Millionaire Next Door, published in 1996, popularized “millionaire” as a wealth benchmark for a mass audience — but a dollar has lost more than half its value since then (U.S. CPI-U rose about 113.5% from 1996 to 2026, a factor of roughly 2.13×). This tool combines that historical inflation record with your own forward-looking inflation assumption to show what your retirement balance would be worth in 1996 terms — a more honest read on how far it would have gone by that era's standard of “millionaire.”

How the nominal / today's-dollars toggle works

The three balance figures at the top always show all three bases side by side. Everything below the toggle switches together. Under the hood, accumulation (the chart and Milestones table) is computed natively in nominal, future dollars, since that's how salary and account balances actually grow; retirement onward (the Social Security comparison, Year-by-year retirement income, and Legacy & trust) is computed natively in real, today's-dollars terms, since that's the only sensible unit for a target withdrawal rate and RMD math that has to hold over 20–40+ years. The toggle converts between the two by inflating or deflating each figure using your assumed inflation rate and how many years out it falls, so what you see is always internally consistent — never a mix of both.

One figure is worth calling out: in nominal mode, the Social Security comparison table's “lifetime income + legacy” column sums each year's actual future dollars received across decades of different years — a legitimate number (it's what would really land in your accounts over time), but an unusual one to eyeball, since a dollar received at 90 buys far less than a dollar received at 65. For apples-to-apples comparison of purchasing power across your retirement, today's-dollars mode is the more standard lens.

How the post-death trust projection works

Whatever remains across your four accounts at your planning age becomes the trust's starting balance, expressed in today's dollars. Each year, the trust distributes your chosen percentage of its current balance and the remainder grows at the same market return you set for your portfolio, adjusted for inflation (i.e. in real terms, matching how the rest of this tool reports money) — it does not separately model your ESOP's company-specific return, trust or estate taxes, trustee fees, or a step-up in cost basis at death. A fixed percentage-of-balance policy is a common, simple approach real trusts use (the distribution naturally shrinks if the balance shrinks and grows if it grows), but 100 years is an extremely long extrapolation: constant-return, constant-spend-rate projections that far out should be read as illustrating the mechanics of compounding versus spending, not as a forecast anyone should rely on. Real trusts face market volatility, changing tax law, trustee decisions, and family circumstances that no formula this simple can capture.

Other simplifications worth knowing

Contribution limits use 2026 IRS figures ($24,500 401(k)/403(b) elective deferral, $7,500 IRA, with age-based catch-ups) applied as one combined cap across your Traditional and Roth employee contributions — a simplification, since 401(k)/403(b) and IRA limits are technically separate. Investment growth is modeled as a constant annual return rather than year-to-year market volatility, which smooths over sequence-of-returns risk that matters a great deal in the years right before and after retirement — a real risk this tool does not capture. This tool does not account for healthcare costs, long-term care, pensions, home equity, other debts or assets, state or local taxes, or Social Security's spousal/survivor benefit rules.
